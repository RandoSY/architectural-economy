Architectural Economy in Biological and Technological Systems
Architectural economy, shared dependencies and functional continuity: from the PDP-8 to the regulatory genome

Randall S. Young | Independent Researcher, Indiana, USA
ORCID: https://orcid.org/0009-0003-1657-0243
24 November 2026 | Version 7.0 — preprint candidate; not peer reviewed



Abstract
Biological systems and engineered technologies confront a shared class of architectural problems: generating
diverse, reliable behavior under finite material, energetic, and informational resources. We define architectural
economy as the context-dependent capacity to achieve a required portfolio of functions through composable
primitives, reuse, modular organization, and distributed control while accommodating the costs of robustness and
adaptation. The 1965 DEC PDP-8 illustrates how economical hardware supports complex software-defined
behavior through subroutine reuse, while exposing a fundamental trade-off: shared control elements reduce static
implementation cost but concentrate failure liability. In biological systems, conserved molecular machinery and
distributed genomic regulatory networks present a mechanistically distinct yet analytically comparable trade-off.

Two computational benchmarks examine the trade-off: 30 seeded portfolios of matched combinatorial networks
and a PDP-8 instruction-level multiplication benchmark. At 32 synthetic outputs, reuse reduces distinct nodes by
50.9% but increases mean motif-fault impact from 0.96 to 4.08 outputs; targeted buffering reduces the latter to
0.10, or 0.485 under the broader internal-node fault model. At 64 PDP-8 tasks, a shared subroutine saves 70.8% of
code words at a 4.1% modeled memory-cycle cost, but increases mean non-timeout routine-fault impact from 0.93
to 55.82 tasks. Dual modularity and redundant verification reduce this dependency. Wider fault injection reveals
vulnerability in the coordination machinery itself. Exhaustive functional checks cover 16,384 multiplication
results and 540 network comparisons across all 4,096 input states, without discrepancies.

An exploratory genomic pilot tested whether weakly buffered downstream influence predicts noncoding sequence
constraint (H2), combining K562 perturbation profiles, ABC enhancer–gene maps and 241-way phyloP scores (636
regions, 408 genes). The association was not supported: adjusted reach β = −0.026 (95% CI −0.094 to 0.042);
reach–buffering interaction β = 0.012 (−0.056 to 0.080). Held-out prediction deteriorated with added predictors.
We distinguish sequence constraint from functional continuity (H3), grounded in developmental system drift and
the historical precedent of compatible PDP-8 implementations. Functional Equivalence Mapping (H3-M) proposes
measuring sequence divergence against independently assayed activity in primate enhancers, using prespecified
equivalence limits, then testing whether architectural measures predict tolerance. Neither H3 nor H3-M has yet
been tested here.

Keywords: architectural economy; PDP-8; regulatory genomics; evolutionary constraint; functional continuity;
functional equivalence; robustness; developmental system drift.



1. Introduction: complexity under constraint
A central challenge across both biological organization and engineered technology is how finite resources sustain
diverse, reliable, and coordinated behavior. In a computer, constraints include logic gates, core memory, execution
cycles, and energy. In an organism, constraints include molecular synthesis, cellular bioenergetics, developmental
timing, and reproductive fitness. Sharing a class of architectural trade-offs does not imply a shared origin:
engineered systems reflect intentional design under explicit specifications, whereas biological architectures arise
through mutation, selection, genetic drift, and historical contingency [20–24]. Throughout this manuscript, the
correspondence between computing and genomics is treated strictly as functional and analytical—not as a claim
of implementation identity, optimality, or literal software execution in cells.

Our central thesis is that both biological organization and emerging technologies face pressure to achieve
functional diversity with economical use of resources. Architecture—the organization, composition, reuse and
coordination of components—mediates that objective. Reuse expands functionality without a proportional
increase in machinery, but concentrates dependencies. Redundancy can protect these dependencies, yet its
coordinating mechanisms can themselves become critical failure points. This nested trade-off, rather than simple
minimization of components, is the organizing principle of this article. A further question is whether functional
architecture persists as its implementation changes. This distinction links the historical PDP-8 example to the
interpretation of our genomic pilot. A molecular entry point is the existence of alternative sequences that satisfy
the same measurable functional requirements; Sections 7.2–7.3 develop a test of this proposition.

Only a small fraction of the human genome encodes protein sequence. The remainder comprises cis-regulatory
elements, noncoding RNA genes, introns, repetitive DNA, and sequences with no established organism-level
function. While the ENCODE project cataloged widespread biochemical activity across the genome [1], biochemical
detection is not equivalent to evolutionary necessity or causal phenotype [2], and estimates of sequence constraint
depend on evolutionary timescale and neutral background models [3,4]. Distinguishing between specifying
molecular components and coordinating their deployment (timing, localization, and magnitude of expression)
provides a structured entry point for asking why particular noncoding regions experience strong purifying
selection—while acknowledging that protein-coding genes also encode regulatory machinery and that large
fractions of noncoding DNA are non-regulatory.



2. The PDP-8 as a case study in architectural economy
Introduced in 1965, Digital Equipment Corporation’s PDP-8 combined a 12-bit word, a base 4,096-word core
memory, a single accumulator (AC), a 1-bit link register (L), and eight primary opcode classes (AND, TAD, ISZ,
DCA, JMS, JMP, IOT, and OPR) [25,26]. Its OPR class combined compatible microoperations within a single
instruction word, while complex arithmetic—including multiplication and division, which the base hardware
lacked—was constructed from sequences of elementary instructions and reusable subroutines (Figure 1). That
compactness enabled a general-purpose minicomputer at a fraction of contemporary hardware cost, though
economy in one resource frequently shifts cost into another: fewer hardware opcodes increase software length,
execution cycles, and programmer effort.




Figure 1. PDP-8 architectural economy. Simplified stored-program architecture showing the base 4,096-word core memory
configuration, single-accumulator processor, peripheral interface, and eight primary opcode classes [25,26]. AI-assisted illustration;
conceptual schematic, not measured data.

The PDP-8 family demonstrates how an architectural interface can outlast its implementation. The discrete-
transistor PDP-8 (1965), TTL-based PDP-8/I (1968) and MSI-based PDP-8/E (introduced 1970) retained a
compatible core instruction architecture [25,26,36,45]. Intersil subsequently implemented compatible instruction
behavior in its CMOS IM6100 microprocessor [46]. Programs confined to the shared architecture could be reused;
peripheral-specific software, timing and extensions could require adaptation. This is functional invariance within
a defined interface, not identical behavior under all conditions (Table 1; Figure 11).

3. Architectural economy as a general systems principle
Herbert Simon’s analysis of hierarchical, nearly decomposable systems [27] provides the theoretical bridge
between the PDP-8 and the genome. Hierarchies make complex organization tractable when interactions within
modules are stronger than interactions across modules. Kirschner and Gerhart’s conserved, versatile core
processes [20] suggest how biological diversity arises through regulatory recombination. Kitano’s framework [21]
emphasizes robustness trade-offs, and Castle et al. [22] connect evolvability to engineering design. Architectural
economy integrates these perspectives: economy is a property of an arrangement of dependencies, not simply a
count of parts.

We define architectural economy operationally as achieving a specified portfolio of functions and minimum
reliability within a domain-specific resource budget. Relevant costs cannot be equated numerically across
domains: logic gates, memory words, execution cycles, and power in a processor differ fundamentally from
molecular synthesis, expression burden, developmental time, and fitness costs in an organism. Furthermore, a
system economical in one resource may be expensive in another: redundancy raises static component cost but

lowers expected failure cost, and neutral or weakly deleterious sequences readily persist in multicellular genomes
whenever population-genetic drift overwhelms weak purifying selection [23,24].

Compare architectures that realize a matched functional portfolio using a cost vector (static resources, dynamic
operation, and reliability loss), rather than a single universal currency. An architecture Pareto-dominates another
when it is no worse on every chosen metric and strictly better on at least one. A reused component increases the
number of outputs that may be exposed to one fault; the magnitude depends on the actual function, context,
redundancy and fault distribution. The proposed biological counterpart concerns fitness-relevant perturbations,
not the assumption that all mutations are loss-of-function events.



4. Molecular implementation of economical coordination
Yan et al. compared the transcriptional regulatory network of Escherichia coli with the Linux kernel function-call
graph [5] and revealed a striking topological contrast: software call graphs rely heavily on fan-in (many high-
level callers invoking reusable low-level subroutines), whereas bacterial transcriptional networks exhibit
pronounced fan-out (relatively few regulators controlling many downstream targets) (Figure 2). Rather than
invalidating the architectural comparison, this contrast sharpens it: both fan-in (shared subroutines) and fan-out
(shared regulators) create high-leverage single points of failure whose disruption propagates across many
outputs. In Section 6.1, we test both regimes directly—fan-out in synthetic combinatorial networks and fan-in in a
PDP-8 subroutine benchmark.

conceptual schematic, not measured data.

Closer mechanistic grounding comes from experimentally reconstructed developmental gene regulatory networks
(GRNs), notably in sea urchin embryos, where cis-regulatory modules integrate signaling and transcription-factor
inputs into cell-fate commitments [6–8]. Four interacting molecular layers implement economical, distributed
coordination:

4.1 Sequence-specific combinatorial logic
Transcription factors recognize degenerate DNA motifs in enhancers, promoters, and silencers, integrating
activating and repressing signals in a context-dependent manner [7–9] (Figure 3). Unlike idealized Boolean logic
gates, cis-regulatory responses are graded, concentration-dependent, stochastic, and sensitive to binding-site
syntax and chromatin kinetics. Crucially, the same transcription factor acts across thousands of loci, and a single
enhancer can operate across multiple tissues or developmental stages.

4.2 Spatial organization and dynamic connectivity
Three-dimensional chromatin folding—shaped in part by CTCF and cohesin-mediated loop extrusion [10,11]—
modulates contact probabilities between distal enhancers and target promoters (Figure 4). Because acute cohesin
depletion eliminates loop domains without uniformly abolishing transcription [11], 3D topology acts as a
quantitative, context-sensitive constraint rather than a rigid wiring harness. Combining enhancer biochemical
activity with physical contact frequency yields strong predictive models of enhancer–promoter regulation [12].




Figure 4. Spatial organization of regulatory interactions. Chromatin looping brings a distal enhancer and target promoter into three-
dimensional proximity [10–12]; physical contact alone does not guarantee transcriptional activation. AI-assisted illustration;
conceptual schematic, not measured data.

4.3 RNA-mediated control and persistent regulatory state
Long noncoding RNAs and chromatin modifiers establish persistent, context-specific regulatory states. For
example, Xist recruits SHARP/HDAC3 silencing complexes across the inactive X chromosome [13], while DNA
methylation, histone modifications, and chromatin accessibility stabilize cell-state memory [14]. These layers act
jointly with protein and RNA machinery rather than constituting an autonomous “epigenetic code.”

4.4 Distributed control, feedback, and redundancy
Biological regulation operates through coupled local processes rather than a central supervisory kernel (Figure 5).
Feedback and feedforward motifs shape response dynamics and noise filtering [15]—for example, Hes7-mediated
negative feedback drives vertebrate segmentation-clock oscillations [16]—while overlapping (“shadow”)
enhancers buffer developmental phenotypes against single-element perturbations [17].

Together, these mechanisms represent competing architectural strategies: shared regulators minimize distinct
molecular machinery, modular enhancers allow context-specific deployment, and shadow enhancers add fault
tolerance at the cost of extra regulatory DNA and coordination complexity.



5. Conservation of components versus conservation of coordination
Comparative genomics demonstrates substantial purifying selection outside protein-coding exons. Bejerano et al.
identified 481 ultraconserved elements (at least 200 bp identical across human, mouse and rat), many noncoding
[18]. The Zoonomia 240-mammal analysis estimated that at least 332 million human bases (approximately 10.7%)
are unusually conserved and identified 4,552 nearly perfectly conserved elements [4]. Coding sequence is
generally more constrained per nucleotide; the large quantity of noncoding sequence means many constrained
bases are noncoding. Deleting selected ultraconserved enhancers near Arx produces viable mice with neurological
and developmental abnormalities [19], establishing contributions to normal development for those elements, not
universal essentiality.

Two established lines of work already connect regulatory architecture to conservation: 1. Developmental
regulatory kernels: Davidson and Erwin proposed that deeply conserved subcircuits (“kernels”) resist
evolutionary rewiring because their internal wiring underpins body-plan specification [28]. 2. Enhancer
pleiotropy: Singh and Yi demonstrated that human enhancers active across multiple tissues and linked to more
target genes exhibit stronger sequence conservation [29].

The open question addressed here is narrower: does higher-order downstream coordination, moderated by local
buffering, explain additional variation in sequence constraint beyond conventional enhancer pleiotropy? (Figure
6). A single-context test cannot separate gene-level reach from invariant target-gene identity; a multi-context
design is needed for that contrast.

We formalize two distinct, separately falsifiable hypotheses:

        H1 (architectural economy): Reuse of composable operations can reduce static implementation cost
         relative to functionally equivalent dedicated designs, while increasing shared fault exposure. Modularity
         and targeted redundancy can reduce exposure; the resulting architecture must be evaluated against the
         additional cost and vulnerability of its coordinating machinery.
        H2 (coordination-constraint): Within a defined class of functional noncoding elements, broad, weakly
         buffered higher-order downstream coordination predicts greater sequence constraint after controlling for
         sequence context, enhancer activity breadth, direct target count and invariant target-gene properties.
         This is a prediction about associations under a specified empirical design, not evidence that all noncoding
         DNA is functional.


6. Computational benchmarks and genomic investigation
We separate the general systems hypothesis (H1), examined in controlled computational models, from the
comparative-genomic hypothesis (H2), examined provisionally using public data and a proposed more complete
protocol.

6.1 Controlled computational benchmarks (testing H1)
Two Python benchmarks examine fan-out (regulatory-like reuse) and fan-in (subroutine-like reuse). The v2
implementation and archived JSON data are retained; a separate audit program in audit/reproduce_audit.py
extends functional testing, measures executable capacity, and tests other tracked instructions. All numerical
results are conditional on the stated workloads, architectures and fault models.

Benchmark A: Synthetic multi-output combinatorial networks (fan-out regime)

Matched portfolios contain K = 4, 8, 12, 16, 24 or 32 outputs over 12 binary inputs; 30 seeded portfolios per K were
evaluated on 256 sampled states. Each output integrates two three-input motifs sampled from a Zipf-distributed
library of 16 primitives. Four architectures implement the same portfolio: dedicated private motifs; fully shared
motifs; modular sharing with fan-out capped at four; and modular sharing with disjunctive shadow copies for
motifs serving at least two outputs. Activation cost counts active modeled internal nodes, not biological energy or
gate-level circuit cost. The loss-of-function model forces a motif or output-integrator node to zero; blast radius
counts outputs changed on at least one test input.

6.1.1 Independent implementation audit and coordination liability
The v3 audit (audit/reproduce_audit.py; results/independent_audit.json) exhaustively tests the 64 × 64 six-bit
operand pairs in each architecture, in batches of 64, for 16,384 correct products in total. Across six network sizes
and 30 seeds, each of three reused architectures was also compared against dedicated circuits on every one of the
4,096 binary input states: 540 comparisons with zero output-bit mismatches. This verifies functional equivalence
for the modeled workloads, not complete CPU fidelity or biological validity.

The code-space estimates (92, 388, 385 and 378 tasks) are limited by the actual fixed operand/result layout, which
reserves 128 entries per table. With the deterministic capacity workload in the audit, the longest consecutive
successful runs were 92 tasks inlined and 128 in each reused architecture. At task 129 the reused designs exhibit
output errors; the estimate of 388 tasks therefore cannot be presented as demonstrated workload capacity.

6.2 Comparative genomics: proposed confirmatory design
Testing H2 requires separating higher-order downstream coordination from two major confounders: (1)
conventional enhancer pleiotropy (tissue breadth and direct target count) and (2) direct target-gene identity
(an enhancer linked to a master developmental transcription factor automatically inherits its target gene’s broad
downstream network unless target-gene properties are rigorously controlled).

Operational variable definitions (strictly conservation-free predictors)

Represent each cellular or developmental context c as a directed, weighted regulatory graph G ( c ) linking
candidate enhancers e to direct target genes g (via CRISPRi perturbations or Activity-by-Contact [ABC] scores
[12]) and linking target genes g to downstream transcriptional modules (via independent Perturb-seq /
experimental TF-target regulons, never using sequence-conservation features to define edges or modules). For
each noncoding candidate enhancer e mapped to primary target gene g( e ):

        B ( e ) — Context-specific higher-order downstream reach: Number of distinct downstream co-
         expression or Perturb-seq functional modules reachable in ≥ 2 hops from e through its direct target(s)
         within the specific contexts c where e is active, subject to prespecified edge-confidence and path-length
         thresholds.
        T ( e ) — Enhancer activity breadth (pleiotropy baseline): Number of distinct biosamples or cell
         states in which e exhibits active enhancer chromatin state (e.g., ENCODE cCRE / DNase + H3K27ac [14]).
         To avoid collinearity, activity breadth enters exclusively as T ( e ) and is not duplicated inside covariate
         vector Z .
        R(e) — Local buffering: primary measure should be independently estimated co-active alternate-
         enhancer capacity, ideally functional attenuation from matched single and combined perturbations. Raw
         (unnormalized) activity × contact for alternate elements can serve as a secondary proxy [12,17].

6.3 Exploratory genomic pilot: observed results
Data and cohort construction

The pilot joins the K562-Roadmap subset of the thresholded ABC atlas (score ≥ 0.015) [31] to selected annotated
Perturb-seq profiles [30,32]. The reported count of differentially expressed genes after gene knockdown is a proxy
for downstream reach (B), not a mapped causal cascade or enhancer-specific perturbation. Ambiguous gene
identifiers and tied primary-target scores are excluded. The highest-scoring eligible distal target is assigned to
each ABC-labeled intergenic interval at least 2 kb from its assigned TSS. This produces 695 autosomal intervals
before conservation is examined. Independent exon/promoter exclusion is not yet verified.

The hg19 intervals were converted locally to hg38 with CrossMap using an hg19-to-hg38 chain. Split, duplicate or
length-changing mappings were excluded. Of 695 original IDs, 694 match the corrected BED and conservation TSV
uniquely; 693 changed coordinates and 694 retain their original length. One interval has no score coverage, leaving
693 fully covered regions. The outcome is the mean phyloP over covered bases from the UCSC Zoonomia 241-way
track [33]. The supplied mapping and score arithmetic were checked internally; independent chain and BigWig re-
extraction remains necessary. An earlier extraction using unconverted coordinates was invalid and is excluded
entirely.

Analysis

Complete-case analysis retains 636 regions, 408 target genes, 22 autosomes. We fit ordinary least squares with
target-gene-clustered standard errors. B is log(1 + reported DE count); R is log(1 + summed raw ABC activity–
contact numerators of other thresholded distal links to the same gene). R is a provisional alternate-enhancer
proxy, not measured redundancy. Seven baseline covariates are log-transformed region length, distance, focal
activity, direct target count, perturbed cell count and UMI count, plus residual expression. Predictors are
standardized, and the interaction is the product of standardized B and R. Leave-one-chromosome-out prediction
fits preprocessing using training chromosomes only. No preregistration, formal power calculation or correction
for multiple exploratory tests was conducted.

Cohort flow: 695 original intervals; 694 unique mapped; 693 fully covered; 636 complete cases. The unadjusted and

6.4 Predictions and falsification criteria
     P1 (supported within the computational models): Reuse reduces static implementation costs and
        amplifies selected shared-component perturbation impacts. Modularity and redundancy can reduce those
        impacts, but their relative benefit depends on the fault model and explicit cost of coordination.
     P2 (not supported by the exploratory pilot): Higher-order downstream reach predicts stronger sequence
        constraint, with an attenuating buffering interaction after relevant covariate control. Test the reach main
        effect between genes in single-context data; within-gene estimation requires genuinely context-varying
        reach.
     P3 (Perturbation signature): Matched high-reach, low-buffering enhancers produce broader
        downstream transcriptional disturbances upon CRISPRi perturbation than low-reach or strongly buffered
        enhancers of comparable activity.
     P4 (out-of-sample predictive gain): Global M2 improves absolute outcome prediction over global M1 on
        held-out chromosomes; within-gene M2b improves within-gene contrast prediction over M1b. The second
        task does not predict an unseen gene intercept. Report performance and uncertainty separately across
        sensitivity checks.

A sufficiently precise null or opposite association in a well-powered, appropriately measured test would count
against H2; failure due to inadequate data coverage, poor proxy validity or lack of within-gene variation would
remain inconclusive. The computational findings about H1 do not imply H2.

7. Limitations and alternative explanations
Several biological and methodological factors constrain interpretation:

    1.   Non-coordination drivers of sequence conservation: Evolutionary constraint reflects purifying
         selection, local mutation-rate variation, and historical contingency. High noncoding conservation can
         arise from overlapping RNA processing signals, dosage sensitivity, structural chromatin roles, or single
         indispensable developmental transitions—none of which require broad multi-module reach.
    2.   Binding-site turnover and sequence decoupling: compensatory changes can preserve enhancer output
         despite changes to its sequence [34]; comparative mammalian studies document rapid enhancer activity
         turnover [35]. Conserved coordination therefore need not imply conservation at the nucleotide level. This
         provides an alternative explanation, not a demonstrated cause of our null result.
    3.   Ascertainment bias and static graph limits: Well-studied developmental regulators appear artificially
         central in incomplete perturbation networks. Moreover, topological reachability in a static graph
         approximates—but does not replace—time-resolved dynamical sensitivity; quantitative Perturb-seq
         effect sizes are essential complements.
    4.   Non-universal buffering effects: Redundant (“shadow”) enhancers do not universally relax sequence
         constraint; multiple enhancers often experience independent purifying selection because their buffering
         roles diverge across environmental stresses or developmental temperatures [17].
    5.   Population-genetic drift limits: weakly selected costs can be overwhelmed by drift, particularly in small
         effective populations [23,24]. The relative evolutionary importance of extra nucleotides, expression costs
         and developmental perturbation remains an empirical question; architecture may be economical along
         some axes but not others.
    6.   Structural limits of the computing analogy: As demonstrated directly in our PDP-8 fault-injection
         benchmark, stored-program computers couple tasks through a shared sequential program counter ( PC)
         and separate code from execution hardware. Genomes lack a central kernel, encode the very machinery
         that reads them, and self-assemble from inherited cellular states.


7.1 Functional continuity: a refined hypothesis (H3)
The PDP-8 case distinguishes an invariant functional interface from changing physical implementations. Its
biological counterpart is developmental system drift (DSD): homologous traits can retain their phenotype while
their developmental or genetic basis diverges [43,44]. Here, three measurements must remain distinct: (i)
nucleotide conservation, (ii) regulatory activity and (iii) coordinated responses. Selection acts through fitness
consequences, not a count of downstream transcripts. Our K562 analysis addresses sequence constraint, not cross-
species continuity.

Experimental precedents include compensatory evolution of a Drosophila enhancer [34], mammalian enhancer
turnover [35], nonhomologous regulatory elements supporting conserved neuronal Pomc expression [37], and
expression stability associated with complex regulatory landscapes [38]. DSD is not a newly discovered
phenomenon [43,44]. Our proposed contribution is to test whether measured architectural dependency and
buffering explain variation in functional continuity beyond established predictors. Unlike deliberate engineering,
evolution has no specified instruction set or external designer; functional equivalence is conditional on biological
context.

H3 (functional continuity hypothesis). Among homologous biological modules compared in matched contexts,
greater fitness-relevant coordination demands predict greater conservation of perturbational input–output
behavior, after accounting for regulatory activity breadth, expression, essentiality and phylogeny. Molecular
sequences and individual network edges may change while this behavior persists. The hypothesis does not require
every important module to remain unchanged, nor does it assert that all sequence turnover preserves function.

solely on activity–contact proxies.

and falsification criteria. Failure to improve prediction of functional continuity in a sufficiently powered test
would count against H3; stable output alone would not establish conserved internal wiring. No cross-species
perturbational analysis has yet been performed here.

7.2 Molecular functional equivalence (H3-M)
Sequence substitution and the functional contract

The same function can be realized by more than one nucleotide sequence. In coding DNA, synonymous
substitutions preserve the amino-acid sequence but may alter expression or splicing; conservative amino-acid
substitutions can preserve a biochemical activity without guaranteeing identical protein function. In noncoding
DNA, changes in binding sites, compensatory substitutions and enhancer turnover can preserve regulatory
activity [34,35,37,39,40]. The analogy to the PDP-8 is a compatible functional interface, not an assumption that
genes execute software. Molecular equivalence is always relative to a specified assay, cellular context and
tolerance; it does not imply equivalence of whole organisms.

We propose Functional Equivalence Mapping (FEM) to characterize this many-to-one relationship. For
corresponding sequences s and t, let d(s,t) be substitutions per comparable aligned base (indels reported
separately), and let Δ = A(t) − A(s), where A is measured log₂ reporter enrichment in a common experimental
context. The pair satisfies the operational activity requirement when the true Δ lies between − δ and + δ, where δ
is an independently justified maximum tolerable difference. Equivalence within a tolerance is a pairwise relation,
not necessarily transitive. Figure 12 shows how molecular divergence and assay-level equivalence are
distinguished.




Figure 12. Functional Equivalence Mapping (FEM). (A) The shaded band denotes a prespecified activity tolerance independent of

Illustrative design, not experimental measurements [41].

H3-M: among active, comparable regulatory sequences, independently measured buffering and alternative
regulatory implementations should predict activity equivalence beyond sequence divergence and conventional
covariates. The prediction is conditional: extra coordinating dependencies may offset the benefit of redundancy.
This is a molecular subhypothesis of H3; activity equivalence alone cannot demonstrate preservation of network
coordination.

7.3 Prospective research program: from sequence to coordination
Study A: primate enhancer equivalence

Klein et al. [39] assayed 11 extant primate orthologs and nine reconstructed ancestral versions of human-active
enhancer tiles in HepG2 cells, obtaining activity scores for 5,426 sequences across 344 tiles. They already
quantified sequence–activity divergence; FEM is not a claim of priority for this comparison. The proposed
extension is explicit equivalence testing followed by held-out evaluation of architectural predictors.
Supplementary Table S4 and GEO GSE113978 provide the starting resources. Whalen et al. [40] identified opposing
predicted effects of substitutions in human-accelerated regions and tested regulatory activity in human and
chimpanzee neural progenitor cells, providing a complementary context for evaluating candidate compensation.

Freeze the sequence alignment policy, cohort, activity filters, equivalence margin, and precision criteria before
testing. Use replicate-level measurements where available; barcodes are not independent biological replicates.
Estimate paired activity differences with replicate-aware uncertainty, use two one-sided tests (TOST) [41], and
report equivalent, meaningfully different and inconclusive pairs separately. If summary scores lack valid
uncertainty, restrict the analysis to descriptive candidates. Account for related sequences and overlapping tiles
using locus-level resampling and phylogenetically informed models. A high-divergence subset must be defined in
advance. Report ascertainment of human-active liver candidates and uncertainty in reconstructed ancestors;
neither inactive sequences nor unmeasured pairs are evidence of successful equivalence.

Test candidate compensation with measured ancestral, single-substitution and combined-substitution constructs
where available. Restoration toward ancestral activity supports compensation; nonadditive epistasis requires
departure from an explicitly defined additive expectation. Reporter assays isolate aspects of sequence activity but
do not establish endogenous target regulation. Where independent motif multiplicity, enhancer redundancy or
perturbational measurements are available, compare a baseline model (divergence, activity, length, assay context
and phylogeny) against a model augmented with architecture. Hold out entire enhancer loci or clades; avoid using
activity outcomes to construct predictors. Treat absence of predictive improvement in a well-powered analysis as
evidence against H3-M.

Study B: coordination across biological systems

The next level is comparative input–output testing. The cross-species immune-response dataset of Hagai et al. [42]
supplies a prospective starting point for comparing independently defined pathway responses and activation
timing under matched stimuli. Match cell populations, time points and donor-level replicates; compare module
agreement to expression-, size- and detectability-matched controls and within-species reliability. Equivalent
responses alone do not establish preserved causal wiring. Test matched regulator perturbations where suitable
data exist, and then ask whether independently estimated architectural reach and buffering predict cross-species
response continuity beyond established covariates. These are new experiments, not results of the present study.

8. Conclusion: conservation of architecture rather than implementation
Architectural economy provides a shared framework for understanding how complex functionality can arise
under finite resources. In the PDP-8 and synthetic network benchmarks, composable reuse reduces static
implementation cost but concentrates shared dependencies. Modularization and targeted redundancy mitigate
selected failures, yet the machinery that coordinates protection introduces dependencies of its own. Economy,
resilience and adaptability must therefore be assessed together, under explicit resource and fault models. These
are demonstrated properties of the computational models, not claims of universal evolutionary optimization.

Our exploratory genomic analysis did not establish the predicted relationship between downstream regulatory
reach and individual enhancer sequence conservation. The additional predictors did not improve held-out
performance. This result neither establishes H2 nor demonstrates that enhancer turnover caused its failure. It
does, however, clarify a critical distinction: architectural importance cannot be inferred from nucleotide
conservation alone.

The PDP-8 makes the distinction concrete: from discrete transistors through integrated circuits to a CMOS
processor, compatible implementations retained core instruction behavior, allowing compatible software to
survive hardware change [25,26,36,45,46]. What endured was the operational contract, not the circuitry.
Developmental system drift provides the biological counterpart [43,44]: conserved traits can arise through altered
molecular mechanisms. The analogy is functional, not causal; engineering specifies compatibility deliberately,
whereas evolution does not.

The resulting research question is whether architectural organization predicts the range of alternative
implementations that can sustain a specified function. FEM provides a molecular starting point through
equivalence tests and compensation studies. Comparative stimulus–response and perturbation tests then ask
whether coordination itself survives change, beyond conserved baseline output. The proposed H3-M and H3 tests
must distinguish equivalent outputs from equivalent causal dependencies and establish predictive value against
conventional evolutionary explanations. They remain prospective, not confirmation of H2 or evidence of universal
optimality.

The enduring property of a complex system may reside not in the permanence of its components, but in the
continuity of their coordination.
